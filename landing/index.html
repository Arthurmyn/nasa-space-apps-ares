Plan one walk on Mars

ARES brings NASA Mars data into one local view and proposes a candidate route between science stops. Time, climb and the limit for walking back sit next to it. Every number says where it came from.

The problem

Mars data is scattered, and none of it plans a walk

Data about Mars lives across missions, instruments, formats, coordinate systems, resolutions and dates. Public tools such as Mars Trek and the USGS–NASA geologic maps are built for regional and global browsing. A plan assembled from separate layers can pass every single check and still fail as a whole.

  • Fits the clock, not the way back

    A stop fits the EVA time budget but lies beyond the limit for walking back to the habitat.

  • Gentle on one grid, steep on another

    A slope looks mild on a 463 m grid and turns steep on a 1 m terrain model.

  • Layers that do not line up

    Misaligned layers create false hazards, or false "safe" corridors.

The question ARES is built around: can the crew walk back from every point of the route? That check is the heart of the planner.

How it works

From science stops to a checked candidate route

  1. 01

    Choose stops

    Place up to six science targets on the map. Each has an objective, its evidence, a confidence level and a rover image with credit.

  2. 02

    Combine the layers

    Orbital imagery, terrain model, slope, thermal inertia and rover images in one window, with resolution and coverage shown for each.

  3. 03

    Get a candidate route

    An A* search on a 1 m grid runs from the habitat through your stops and back. If no route exists, ARES names the blocking constraint. It never draws a straight line.

  4. 04

    Check the limits

    Each leg gets its 3-D distance, climb, time and a relative effort index, checked against the EVA time budget and the walk-back limit. A marker shows the point of no return.

If no route exists, the planner says which constraint blocks it. Unknown terrain is penalised, never treated as free to cross.

Trust

Every number shows where it came from

Each number on screen carries a value, a unit, a source and one of four labels. If a number has no source or label, it is a bug.

Measured
Observed by an instrument.
Model
Computed from a documented model.
Assumption
A value we chose and name, with its source.
Proxy
Lunar or 1 g data applied to Mars. Never called validated.
  • It is a candidate route, not a safety certification.
  • Effort is a relative index, not an oxygen or metabolism forecast.
  • Hypotheses are labelled as hypotheses.
  • No real-time weather or radiation. Climate figures are historical.

The site

One place, done properly: the boxwork on Mount Sharp

The first area of interest is GALE-BX, the boxwork on Mount Sharp in Gale crater. Search frame: 137–138° E, 5.5–4.3° S.

  • Data on hand. Curiosity has surface radiation and weather records here, thousands of Mastcam and Navcam images, and detailed orbital imagery.
  • Terrain that forces a route. Boxwork ridges are 5–9 m wide and 1–2 m high, so the best path is not a straight line across a plain.
  • A clear science question. Diagenesis along fractures in the sulfate-bearing layers, which record groundwater history.

What we do not claim

We do not claim that ridge and hollow chemistry differ. Recent work found no chemical difference, so targets are framed as "study diagenesis along fracture X".

One area only. A second site is outside the scope of this build.

Who it is for

Built around the people who plan and walk

  • Mission planner

    Picks targets and gets a candidate route. Sees data coverage, a cost breakdown per leg and, when no route exists, the constraint that blocks it.

  • Astronaut or field explorer

    Sees the next point, its distance and a first-person rover image. The briefing repeats only computed numbers.

  • Science specialist

    Gets the objective, value estimate, evidence type, confidence and sources for each target, split into measured, interpreted and hypothesised.

  • Anyone with the link

    No login. Our goal: plan a full walk in under three minutes and point to the source of any number.

Honest status

What works today, and what does not yet

Working in the prototype

On sample data, not real terrain.

  • Click the map to place a start and stops, then drag to move them
  • Route recomputes after each edit, and the old one fades until the new one arrives
  • Slope classes, hazard zones and an elevation profile linked to the map
  • Compare an alternative route
  • Printable one-page route card
  • HUD view, clearly marked as a mockup

In progress

Needed before the planner uses real data.

  • Terrain model clipped to the site and slope, roughness and hazard masks
  • Routing engine with waypoint ordering
  • EVA time budget, walk-back limit and point of no return
  • A source and label on every number, enforced by a test

Planned if time allows

Cut from the bottom of this list first.

  • Sensitivity presets: shortest, least effort, best data confidence
  • Oxygen per leg, shown as an illustration
  • Radiation and historical weather cards
  • Monte Carlo uncertainty over terrain noise
  • GeoJSON and CSV export, line of sight to the habitat, simplified field view

Credit and limits

What is new, and what is not

SEXTANT (MIT) already did metabolic A* routing, separate astronaut and rover energy, and a best path home. We do not present that as new, and we build on that lineage.

What ARES adds:

  1. Several Mars mission datasets combined on one boxwork site.
  2. A walk-back limited by consumables with a margin, and a point-of-no-return marker.
  3. Route uncertainty from terrain noise, with a source label on every number.
  4. A science value model for targets that is tied to the route.

Out of scope

  • Certifying a route as safe
  • Real-time weather or radiation
  • Absolute oxygen or metabolism forecasts
  • A radiation map or dose model by elevation
  • Rover energy and multi-sol planning
